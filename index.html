What Nvidia Really Owns

The most important thing about the world's leading AI chip company may not be the chip.

When Donald Trump defended Nvidia's extraordinary technological position in a November 23 interview with CNBC, he offered an explanation that was both intuitively appealing and profoundly incomplete. The company's chips, he suggested, were so complex that they could not be copied. He also implied that its competitors might be years away from catching up.[1]

There is a reassuring simplicity to this account. If the source of American leadership in artificial intelligence is an impossibly intricate piece of silicon, then the task is to preserve that lead and keep the most valuable chips out of the wrong hands. It is an appealing story about technological power: one company has made something no one else knows how to make.

But suppose a competitor could make an equally good chip tomorrow. Would the balance of power in AI change overnight? Probably not. Now suppose every major AI framework, software library and distributed computing tool could switch effortlessly between competing chips, with comparable performance and reliability. Even if Nvidia retained the best chip on the market, its position would be profoundly different.

The contrast suggests that we are looking in the wrong place for the full explanation of Nvidia's power. Its products are remarkable. But its deeper achievement was to become part of the infrastructure through which other people pursue their own technological achievements.

01 / Silicon

The wrong meaning of copying

There are at least three different things one might mean by copying Nvidia. One is reproducing a particular processor: its circuitry, layout, memory interfaces and other proprietary engineering. Another is developing a different processor capable of performing the same computations with comparable speed and efficiency. A third is reproducing the collection of technologies and institutions that make Nvidia's processors commercially indispensable.

These are different challenges. The first is formidable. A Blackwell GPU contains 208 billion transistors across two interconnected dies and depends on sophisticated fabrication and packaging.[2] Discovering a design would not give a rival the machinery, process knowledge, memory supply or production yields necessary to manufacture it economically.

But the second challenge does not require solving the first. Nobody needs to reproduce the circuitry of an Nvidia GPU to build an effective AI accelerator. Many of the mathematical operations required by modern neural networks are well understood: matrix multiplication, attention, reductions and other forms of numerical computation. Different architectures can implement these operations differently, sometimes with greater efficiency for a particular workload.

Complexity is not a proof of irreplaceability. Nor is a lead in one generation of hardware evidence of a fixed lead of five or ten years. The third challenge, however, is a different matter. To understand it, we need to go back to a time when AI was not the dominant concern of the technology industry.

02 / History

A bet made before the boom

Nvidia began with graphics. Rendering images and video games demands enormous numbers of relatively similar operations that can be carried out simultaneously. Graphics processing units, or GPUs, are built to exploit this parallelism. It eventually became clear that many scientific calculations could benefit from the same approach.

In 2006, Nvidia unveiled CUDA, a programming platform that made its GPUs accessible for purposes far beyond graphics.[3] Rather than requiring researchers to disguise scientific problems as graphical ones, CUDA allowed them to use familiar programming concepts to exploit GPU computation directly.

This was a consequential investment made long before the commercial explosion of generative AI. Nvidia did not merely wait for researchers to find a use for its processors. It provided the means for them to discover new uses. Scientific computing, numerical simulation and machine learning all became part of a growing community with a common technological foundation.

As deep learning accelerated, that earlier investment became more valuable. A generation of researchers had learned to use Nvidia hardware, and the tools on which their work depended had been refined through years of practical experience. Nvidia's later success was not simply a lucky encounter between a graphics company and a new market. It was the consequence of having built a bridge between the two before the new market was fully apparent.

03 / Software

The invisible infrastructure

To understand what a chip company sells in the age of AI, consider how a modern researcher actually trains a neural network. Few researchers design the machine instructions that carry out every operation. Most work through frameworks such as PyTorch, which express mathematical operations at a higher level of abstraction. Underneath the framework sit compilers, numerical libraries, device drivers and communication systems that translate those operations into work performed by the hardware.

CUDA is a critical part of Nvidia's stack. So are cuBLAS, which accelerates linear algebra; cuDNN, which provides optimized neural-network operations; NCCL, which coordinates communication among GPUs; and TensorRT, which helps optimize models for inference. Each layer contains years of engineering work. Together they make it possible for developers to concentrate on their own problems rather than reinvent the mechanisms of computation.

In a PyTorch program, moving a model to a GPU can appear as simple as writing model.to("cuda"). Yet the simplicity of that instruction conceals a vast amount of work. It is a point at which a programmer's intention meets an existing infrastructure.

It would be a mistake to conclude that Nvidia owns PyTorch. The framework originated at Meta and is governed through the PyTorch Foundation, established in 2022 with founding members that included Nvidia, AMD, Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon Web Services.[4] PyTorch can run on hardware other than Nvidia's, and AMD's ROCm implementation even reuses PyTorch's CUDA-named interfaces in many cases.[5] The name in the code is therefore not by itself evidence that a program can only run on Nvidia hardware.

The important question is not who owns the framework. It is whose hardware the larger body of software is most mature on, whose tools developers know best, and on whose systems the next experimental model is most likely to work without unpleasant surprises.

A company does not have to own the applications of the future if it can become the platform on which people build them.

04 / Economics

How excellence becomes market power

Imagine the director of an AI laboratory choosing between two accelerators. One is familiar, supported by the laboratory's existing code and used by its collaborators. The other has a lower price and might even be faster. But adopting it requires engineers to migrate software, test third-party libraries, verify numerical behavior, retrain staff and diagnose failures in a less familiar environment.

The advertised price of the second accelerator is not the price of switching to it. The laboratory must also pay for the transition and accept the risk of delays. If a large training run is interrupted by a software problem, or if an important new model architecture requires an operation that is poorly supported, a modest hardware saving can quickly become irrelevant.

This is how technological excellence becomes an economic barrier. Developers prefer hardware for which good software already exists; software developers prefer hardware with a large installed base. Cloud providers want to offer the equipment their customers request. Researchers publish code that their colleagues can reproduce. All of these choices are individually reasonable, yet collectively they reinforce the same incumbent.

The OECD's 2025 study of competition in AI infrastructure identifies Nvidia's early lead, performance and CUDA ecosystem as important factors in its position, and emphasizes the difficulties that proprietary interfaces can create for customers trying to change hardware.[6] This is not a claim that every customer's decision is forced or that Nvidia's commercial success was unearned. It is a claim about how a market can evolve after a company makes genuinely useful innovations.

There is a further complication. Much of the software that enhances Nvidia's platform was not written by Nvidia. It came from researchers, universities, independent developers and other firms, often through open-source projects. Their contributions can increase the value of a proprietary platform without being proprietary themselves. A commons of shared knowledge and a concentrated market for hardware can coexist. Indeed, the first may help sustain the second.

The closest historical analogy may be Microsoft's position in personal computing. The advantage of Windows was not that Microsoft made the best physical computer. It was that a vast collection of applications and skills had accumulated around its operating system. The analogy is imperfect—Nvidia sells extraordinarily advanced physical products—but it captures an important aspect of its power.

05 / Industry

The chip is not the computer

The emphasis on CUDA should not lead us to the opposite mistake of treating semiconductor design as incidental. Modern AI is constrained not just by how rapidly a processor can do arithmetic but by how efficiently it can receive data, store intermediate results and communicate with other processors. A large model may be distributed over thousands of accelerators, and the system is only as useful as its ability to coordinate their work.

This is why Nvidia's networking technologies, including NVLink and its InfiniBand and Ethernet products, matter so much. Memory bandwidth, interconnects, power delivery, cooling and reliability can determine the economics of a large AI installation. Nvidia has increasingly designed these components as parts of integrated computing systems. Its advantage is not merely the quality of a GPU or even the quality of CUDA in isolation; it is the quality of their integration.

But Nvidia does not fabricate its own leading-edge GPUs. It designs them and depends on companies such as TSMC to manufacture them. Blackwell, for example, uses a customized TSMC process.[2] Advanced memory, packaging, substrates, semiconductor manufacturing equipment and other inputs depend on a wider industrial network. Nvidia's own regulatory disclosures acknowledge its dependence on outside manufacturers and suppliers and describe its commitments to secure production capacity.[7]

That network is itself a source of competitive power. A rival with an elegant design still needs to obtain the components and manufacturing capacity to deliver it at scale. An incumbent with enormous purchasing commitments and established industrial relationships is well positioned to navigate these constraints. But this is industrial coordination, not a physical law. Nor is it a capability confined within the borders of one nation.

Recognizing the sophistication of the whole system gives Nvidia more credit, not less. It has solved a more difficult business problem than merely designing a fast processor: it has made many independently difficult technologies work together at industrial scale.

06 / Competition

The competition that already exists

The clearest objection to the proposition that Nvidia's technology cannot be meaningfully replicated is that leading AI systems already run on alternative hardware.

Google developed its Tensor Processing Units, or TPUs, for machine learning. Its technical report on the original Gemini models documents the use of Google's own TPUs for training.[8] These are not attempts to reproduce Nvidia's GPU designs. They are alternative architectures intended to accomplish many of the same tasks.

Amazon has developed its own Trainium accelerators. Anthropic, the developer of Claude, has publicly described training and serving its models across Nvidia GPUs, Google TPUs and Amazon Trainium, rather than confining its computing strategy to a single chip platform.[9]

AMD is pursuing a more direct challenge through its Instinct GPUs and ROCm software. The November 2026 MLPerf training results include submissions based on AMD, Nvidia and Google hardware, among others.[10] Such benchmarks are useful evidence that competition is real, although comparing unlike system sizes or workloads cannot establish which chip is universally superior. Time to a target level of model quality, power consumption, utilization, purchase or rental price and operational reliability all matter.

The distinction between training and inference also matters. Training novel models benefits from flexibility and mature software. Inference—the repeated execution of an already trained model—can sometimes be optimized for a narrower range of operations. Specialized accelerators may be especially attractive when the computation is predictable and the volume is enormous, although the growing complexity of reasoning models makes inference a moving target too.

Google and Amazon illustrate another point. Large cloud companies have reasons and resources to design their own chips that a small startup lacks. They can spread development costs across vast internal workloads and build their own software systems. They also have incentives to reduce reliance on a supplier whose pricing and product allocation affect their businesses. But replacing Nvidia with a handful of proprietary cloud platforms would not necessarily produce an open, competitive computing market. It could merely relocate the dependency.

07 / Policy

A dangerous confusion about national security

The distinction between copying a design and reproducing a capability is especially important in debates over exports to China. If the goal of an export restriction is to delay another country's acquisition of advanced AI capabilities, then the relevant question is not whether the receiving country can reproduce the exact transistor layout of an imported chip. The question is what that country can do with the chips it receives, and what it can eventually do without them.

Even a chip that cannot be reverse-engineered economically can still be used for computation. A buyer need not understand how to manufacture a supercomputer to run it. Access to accelerators may increase the capacity to develop advanced AI even when the underlying hardware remains impossible for the buyer to reproduce. The assertion that a chip cannot be copied therefore does not, on its own, justify exporting it.

Conversely, restrictions on advanced fabrication equipment, memory and packaging capacity may constrain the development of alternatives even when their designs are technically viable. China's domestic accelerator efforts, including Huawei's Ascend line, should be assessed in terms of usable computing systems, manufacturing scale and software support, not simply their resemblance to Nvidia's products.

There is also a serious argument in the other direction: maintaining access to Nvidia's ecosystem in overseas markets may reinforce American technological influence and make domestic alternatives less attractive. Broad restrictions can create incentives for rival ecosystems to mature. Those considerations deserve to be weighed against the capabilities enabled by exports and the risks associated with them. Neither side of that debate is settled by pronouncing the hardware uncopyable.

The policy problem is dynamic. The objective might be to preserve a technological lead, limit certain military applications, maintain commercial influence, or reduce dependence on geographically concentrated production. These goals overlap, but they are not identical and may sometimes conflict. An export policy should be judged according to its effects on the goals it is supposed to serve, rather than the rhetorical force of an assertion about chip complexity.

08 / The future

What would actually break the moat?

Suppose a competitor announces a processor that beats Nvidia on one benchmark by twenty percent. This is noteworthy, but it tells us little about whether developers will migrate. Now suppose a competitor produces a comparable accelerator at a much lower total cost and can run existing applications reliably with little engineering work. That development is much more consequential.

The most interesting threat may not be a single rival chip. It may be a change in the relationship between software and hardware. Frameworks, compilers and portable kernel languages are increasingly trying to allow programmers to express computations once and execute them efficiently across different architectures. Where these efforts succeed, developers no longer have to make as many hardware-specific commitments at the beginning of a project.

Perfect portability is unlikely. Hardware differs for good reasons, and specialized optimization can deliver substantial performance improvements. Nor is it sufficient for an application merely to run on several devices. It must run well, be easy to debug, retain the required numerical behavior, and perform reliably at scale. Portability should be evaluated empirically rather than inferred from the existence of a common programming interface.

There are several ways to make competition more meaningful without denying Nvidia the rewards of its innovation. Public research grants and procurement can encourage testing and deployment on multiple architectures. Open interfaces and portable numerical libraries can reduce unnecessary migration costs. Independent performance evaluations can compare complete systems at equivalent quality, including energy use, real-world operating costs and reliability. Competition authorities can examine practices that prevent customers from choosing rival products on their merits, without presuming that popularity or integration is itself unlawful.

These efforts would benefit researchers and businesses even if Nvidia continued to make the best products. They would help establish a distinction between choosing Nvidia because it is the best available option and choosing Nvidia because other options are impractical to use.

There is an economic difference between a superior product and a market in which superiority is difficult to challenge. In the first case, the producer has to continue earning its position. In the second, the producer's position can survive a deterioration in its relative performance. A healthy computing industry should be able to reward Nvidia's achievements without making their consequences permanent.

09 / Conclusion

The thing worth protecting

Nvidia's accomplishment is extraordinary. It made a long-term investment in programmable parallel computing, cultivated a developer ecosystem, advanced semiconductor design and built systems that made large-scale AI practical. Its leadership is neither an illusion nor adequately explained by political connections. But it is also not the discovery of a secret mathematical or physical principle unavailable to the rest of humanity.

What makes the company so difficult to compete with is that its work has become embedded in the work of others. Changing a piece of hardware is one thing. Changing the accumulated knowledge, software, organizational practices and physical infrastructure of an industry is quite another.

This should change how we think about technological leadership. A nation's most valuable asset may not be a particular company's ability to stay ahead of every rival indefinitely. It may instead be an environment in which researchers and firms can keep producing innovations, competing architectures can emerge, and discoveries can spread without being unnecessarily tied to a single supplier.

We should want Nvidia to continue making extraordinary chips. We should also want other companies to be capable of making better ones—and for the people building the next generation of AI to be genuinely free to use them.

The achievement worth protecting is not the impossibility of catching up. It is the capacity to keep moving forward.
